DPI explained: when 300 matters and when it means nothing

DPI is the most misunderstood number in photography. On a screen it is meaningless. On paper it decides everything.

Sizing 11 August 2026 ·6 min read

Someone asks for a photo at 300 DPI. You open the file, find a number saying 72, and panic. In almost every case, nothing is wrong.

What DPI actually describes

DPI — dots per inch — is a ratio between pixels and physical distance. It only means something once the image exists in the physical world.

An image file is a grid of pixels. 1200 × 1800 pixels is 1200 × 1800 pixels, full stop. The DPI tag is a note attached to the file saying "when printed, pack this many pixels into each inch". Change the tag without changing the pixels and you have changed nothing about the image, only the suggested print size.

On screens it is irrelevant

A screen does not use the DPI tag. It has its own fixed pixel density and simply shows the pixels you gave it. An image tagged 72 DPI and the same image tagged 300 DPI are, on screen, identical — same file size, same appearance, same everything.

This is why web requirements asking for "300 DPI images" are usually written by someone repeating a print convention. What they need is enough pixels.

On paper it decides the quality

Print is where it becomes real. The arithmetic is simple:

pixels ÷ DPI = inches, and inches × DPI = pixels.

300 DPI became the standard because it is roughly where a normal person stops being able to see individual dots at reading distance. It is a rule of thumb about human eyes, not a law.

When less than 300 is fine

Viewing distance changes everything. A poster seen from two metres away looks perfect at 150 DPI. A billboard might be 20 DPI and look flawless from the road. The further away it is viewed, the fewer dots per inch you need.

Only things held in the hand — photographs, books, document photos — really demand 300.

What to do when a form asks for 300 DPI

  1. Work out the physical size they want — say 35 × 45 mm.
  2. Convert to inches — 1.38 × 1.77 in.
  3. Multiply by 300 — about 413 × 531 pixels.
  4. Make sure your photo has at least that many pixels.

Working in print sizes directly skips the arithmetic: state the physical size and let the tool work out the pixels. Snug does this when you resize by print size.

The one thing not to do

Do not "increase the DPI" of a small photo and expect it to print better. Re-tagging a 600 × 900 image as 300 DPI just tells the printer to produce a 2 × 3 inch print. If you force it to 4 × 6, the software invents the missing pixels and the result is soft. Detail that was never captured cannot be recovered.

The short version

DPI only exists on paper. For screens, count pixels. For print, multiply the inches you need by 300.
