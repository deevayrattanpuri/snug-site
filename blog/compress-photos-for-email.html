How to email photos that actually arrive

Most mail servers stop at around 25 MB, and some stop much earlier. Four phone photos can breach it.

Workflow 21 July 2026 ·5 min read

Email attachment limits have barely moved in fifteen years while phone cameras have got dramatically better. The result is a message that sits in your outbox, or worse, appears to send and quietly bounces.

The limits you are working against

Most consumer mail services cap attachments around 25 MB, and corporate mail servers are frequently stricter — 10 MB is common. Attachments are also encoded for transit, which inflates them by roughly a third, so a 20 MB folder of photos can arrive at the server as 27 MB and be refused.

Assume your real budget is about three quarters of the stated limit.

What size the recipient actually needs

This is the question that settles everything, and the honest answer is usually "much less than you are about to send".

  • Just looking at them? 1200–1600 px on the long edge. On a phone or a laptop this is indistinguishable from the original.
  • Printing a 6 × 4? Around 1800 px on the long edge.
  • Editing or archiving? Send the originals — but use a file-sharing link, not email.

Twelve photos resized for viewing will typically come to under 10 MB in total, against 48 MB untouched.

Resize the set, don't compress each one

Doing twelve photos individually is tedious enough that you will stop doing it. Batch mode applies one setting to the whole selection in a single pass and tells you what the set weighs when it is done, which is the number you actually care about.

Think about the inbox you are sending to

A ten-photo email is ten attachments to download, scroll past and eventually delete. If you are sending more than a handful, a single PDF or a shared link is kinder — and far less likely to be truncated by a mail client.

A quick checklist

  1. Decide what the recipient needs the photos for.
  2. Batch resize to a sensible long edge — 1600 px covers almost everything.
  3. Check the total size of the folder, not the individual files.
  4. Keep under three quarters of the stated limit.
  5. If it is still too big, send a link instead.

Keep your originals

Resizing for email should never cost you the full-resolution photo. Snug writes the smaller copies as new files, so the versions you send are separate from the versions you keep — and if someone later asks for a printable copy, you still have it.

The short version

Assume three quarters of the stated limit, resize to 1600 px on the long edge, and send a link if it is still too big.
