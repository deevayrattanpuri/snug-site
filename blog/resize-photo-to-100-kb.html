How to resize a photo to 100 KB without wrecking it

A hundred kilobytes is a brutal limit for a modern phone photo. Here is what actually has to give, and the order to give it in.

Sizing 12 October 2026 ·6 min read

Your phone takes photos somewhere between 3 MB and 8 MB. A government portal wants 100 KB. That is not a small adjustment — you are being asked to throw away something like 98% of the file and still hand over something a human can recognise.

It is entirely doable. It just matters a great deal which 98% you throw away.

Three dials, not one

Every photo's file size is the product of three things, and most people only ever touch one.

  • Dimensions — how many pixels wide and tall. Halving both quarters the pixel count, and the file size drops roughly in step.
  • Compression quality — how aggressively the JPEG encoder discards detail. This is the dial most apps expose as a slider.
  • Content — how complicated the picture is. A portrait against a plain wall compresses far smaller than a forest in autumn, at identical settings.

The mistake is to drive quality down on its own. A 4000-pixel-wide photo squeezed to 100 KB by quality alone ends up at perhaps quality 15, which produces the blocky, smeared look everyone recognises as "compressed". The same 100 KB spent on a 1000-pixel-wide version of the same photo at quality 75 looks completely fine.

The order that works

  1. Crop first. Cut anything you do not need. Fewer pixels to describe is free savings, and it is the only step that can actually improve the photo.
  2. Resize to the real display size. If it will be viewed in a form field or printed at passport size, 4000 pixels is wasted. Something between 800 and 1600 px on the long edge is plenty for almost every upload.
  3. Then compress. With sensible dimensions, you will usually find the quality setting that lands under 100 KB is high enough to look clean.

Why letting the app do it is better

Working this out by hand means resize, check the size, nudge the quality, check again. Setting a target file size instead reverses the problem: you state the ceiling and the app searches for the combination that fits underneath it.

That is what Snug's target file size row does. Tap 100 KB, and it works backwards to find the dimensions and quality that get there, showing you the before and after so you can judge whether the trade was worth it.

When 100 KB genuinely is not enough

Some photos cannot survive it. A dense, detailed scene — crowds, foliage, fine text — has too much information for the budget, and you will see it fall apart.

If the photo is a scanned document, switch tactics: crop tightly to the page, and consider PNG if the content is mostly text and line art rather than a photograph. If it is a portrait, crop closer. A head-and-shoulders crop at 100 KB looks far better than a full-body shot at the same budget, because there is less to describe.

Check before you submit

Open the finished file at 100% and look at the parts that matter — eyes, text, edges. If the portal is showing a preview, use it. The worst outcome is not a slightly soft photo; it is discovering the problem after a submission deadline has passed.

The short version

Shrink the dimensions before you touch quality. Most 100 KB disasters are full-resolution photos crushed by compression alone.
