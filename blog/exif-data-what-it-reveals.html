What your photos quietly reveal about you

Every photo carries a hidden record: the camera, the moment, and very often the exact coordinates. Here is how to read it and how to remove it.

Privacy 18 August 2026 ·6 min read

A photo file contains the picture, and then it contains a second, invisible document describing how the picture came to exist. That second document is called EXIF metadata, and it is more detailed than most people expect.

What is in there

  • Date and time — to the second, usually with a timezone
  • Device — make and model of the phone or camera
  • Camera settings — aperture, shutter speed, ISO, focal length, flash
  • Orientation — the rotation flag that keeps photos upright
  • GPS coordinates — latitude, longitude and often altitude
  • Software — what edited the file, and sometimes an edit history

Most of it is harmless and genuinely useful. The GPS line is the one worth thinking about.

Why location data matters more than it sounds

A single coordinate on a holiday photo is not much. A pattern is different. Photos taken at home cluster around a precise point; photos taken at work cluster around another; and a school pickup shows up five days a week. Anyone who collects a handful of your images has a map of your routine.

The realistic risk is not dramatic. It is a marketplace listing showing exactly which house the item is in, or a photo in a public forum revealing a home address you never typed anywhere.

Where it survives, and where it doesn't

Major social networks strip most metadata on upload, partly for privacy and partly for file size. That does not make you safe, because plenty of places do not strip it:

  • Photos attached to an email
  • Files uploaded to classified ads and smaller marketplaces
  • Images posted to forums and personal websites
  • Anything sent through a messaging app that preserves the original file
  • Cloud folders shared by link

When to keep it

Do not strip metadata reflexively. It is what lets your gallery sort by date and group by place, what tells an editor which lens was used, and what proves when a photo was taken if that ever matters. For your own library, keep it.

Strip it from copies that leave your control.

Removing it

The reliable way is to use a tool that writes a clean file without the metadata block. Snug's Metadata tool does this, and because everything runs on the device, the location data never travels anywhere in the process — it is read on the phone, removed on the phone, and the clean copy is written to your gallery with the original left intact.

That last point matters. If you strip metadata by overwriting the original, the date and location are gone from your own library too.

A simple rule

Metadata in, metadata out: keep it on the originals you store, remove it from the copies you publish. It costs one extra step and closes a gap most people never knew was open.

The short version

Keep metadata on your originals, strip it from anything you post publicly. The GPS line is the one that matters.
