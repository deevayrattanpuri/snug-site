JPG, PNG or WebP: which should you actually save in?

Three formats, three jobs. Picking the wrong one is why your screenshot looks fuzzy and your photo is 9 MB.

Formats 15 September 2026 ·6 min read

There is no best image format, only a best format for a particular picture. The difference between them comes down to one question: is the image a photograph, or is it made of flat areas and sharp edges?

JPG: for photographs

JPEG compression works by discarding detail the human eye is bad at noticing — mostly fine colour variation. On a photograph, where everything is already a soft gradient of tones, this is remarkably effective and almost invisible.

On a screenshot it is a disaster. Sharp black text on white gets surrounded by faint grey smudges, called ringing artefacts, because the format has no efficient way to describe a hard edge.

JPEG is also lossy and has no transparency. Use it for photographs, and for anything that has to be accepted by every system on earth.

PNG: for sharp edges

PNG is lossless. What goes in comes out, pixel for pixel, no matter how many times you re-save it. It compresses by finding repeated patterns, which works brilliantly on large flat areas of identical colour — screenshots, logos, diagrams, text, line art. It also supports proper transparency.

Point it at a photograph and it falls apart, because a photo has almost no repeated identical pixels. A photo saved as PNG can easily be five to ten times the size of the same photo as JPG, with no visible benefit.

WebP: the modern compromise

WebP does both. It has a lossy mode that beats JPEG by roughly 25–30% at comparable quality, and a lossless mode that generally beats PNG. It supports transparency and animation.

Its only real weakness is acceptance. Every current browser handles it, but plenty of older upload forms, desktop applications and corporate systems do not. For your own website, WebP. For a government portal, do not risk it.

HEIC: excellent, and often unwelcome

Worth naming because your camera may be producing it. HEIC stores roughly JPEG quality in half the space, which is why phones adopted it. Support outside the phone ecosystem is patchy, so it is a great storage format and a poor sharing one.

Quick reference

You haveUseBecause
A photograph for an upload formJPGUniversally accepted, small
A screenshot or scanned textPNGKeeps edges sharp, no smudging
A logo needing transparencyPNGTrue alpha channel
An image for your own websiteWebPSmaller at the same quality
A phone photo you are keepingHEICHalf the size, same quality
A phone photo you are sendingJPGThe recipient can actually open it

The rule that matters most

Whatever you choose, avoid repeated lossy re-saves. Every time a JPG is opened, edited and saved again, it is re-compressed and loses a little more. Keep one high-quality original and export copies from it, rather than editing the copy of a copy.

The short version

Photographs want JPG. Edges and text want PNG. Your own website wants WebP. Avoid re-saving JPGs repeatedly.
