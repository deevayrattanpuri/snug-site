What happens to a JPEG every time you re-save it

Generation loss is real, cumulative and irreversible. It is also easy to avoid once you know where it comes from.

Formats 16 June 2026 ·5 min read

Open a JPEG, rotate it, save it. Open it again, crop it, save it. Do that a dozen times and the photo visibly degrades — not because of anything you did, but because of what JPEG does every time it writes a file.

Why it happens

JPEG is lossy. When it saves, it discards information it judges you will not miss. That judgement is made fresh every single time, on whatever it was handed.

The crucial detail: the second save does not know what the original looked like. It sees the already-damaged version as the truth and discards detail from that. The errors compound, each generation built on the last one's mistakes.

What it looks like

Early generations are invisible. By five or six you start seeing softness in fine detail. By ten or fifteen, edges develop halos, smooth areas break into blocks, and colours in saturated regions drift. It is the same look as a meme that has been screenshotted and reposted a hundred times, because that is exactly what has happened to it.

What triggers a re-save

  • Any edit saved back to a JPG — crop, rotate, brightness, anything
  • Exporting from an app that re-encodes on the way out
  • Uploading to a platform that recompresses (almost all of them)
  • Sharing through messaging apps that compress attachments
  • Converting between formats and back again

A photo that goes phone → messaging app → saved → uploaded → downloaded has been through four generations before anyone has edited it.

What does not cause it

Copying a file does not. Moving it does not. Backing it up does not. Simply opening it to look at it does not. Only writing a new JPEG does — so copying a photo between devices a hundred times costs you nothing at all.

Lossless formats — PNG, and WebP in lossless mode — do not suffer from this. Re-save a PNG a thousand times and it is bit-for-bit identical.

How to avoid it

  1. Keep one untouched original and treat it as the master.
  2. Export from the original every time, rather than editing an export.
  3. Do all your changes in one pass — crop, resize and convert together, so there is only one encode.
  4. Do not re-save at high quality to "fix" a bad file. It cannot recover what is gone, and makes the file larger for nothing.

Why keeping originals matters

This is the practical reason an app should never overwrite your source photo. If every resize replaced the original, your library would quietly degrade with each pass.

The short version

Each JPEG save discards a little more, permanently. Keep one original and export from it every time.
