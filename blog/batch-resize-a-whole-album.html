How to batch resize a whole album without losing your mind

Forty photos, one setting, one pass. The trick is deciding the setting before you start.

Workflow 1 September 2026 ·5 min read

Resizing one photo is a minor chore. Resizing forty, one at a time, is how people give up and send a 200 MB email that bounces.

Decide the destination first

Batch processing goes wrong when you start before knowing what the photos are for. The answer changes everything:

  • Email or messaging — 1200–1600 px on the long edge is generous. Most recipients view on a phone.
  • A web gallery — around 2000 px, saved as WebP if the platform accepts it.
  • Archive storage — do not resize at all. Compress gently if you must.
  • A printed album — leave them alone. Print needs every pixel.

Pick one, then apply it to everything. Mixing destinations in one batch is how you end up redoing the whole thing.

Scale by percentage when sizes vary

If the album is a mix — some panoramas, some portraits, some screenshots — a fixed pixel width treats them unequally. It enlarges the small ones, which helps nobody, and crops the wide ones awkwardly.

Percentage scaling respects each photo's own proportions. 50% makes everything half the size it was, whatever it started as.

Rename on the way out

Camera filenames are chronological but unreadable. A batch rename — italy-01, italy-02 — means the files arrive in order and the recipient can find anything. Doing it during the resize saves a second pass through a file manager.

Check a sample before committing

Run one representative photo through your chosen settings and look at it properly. Pick the most demanding one in the set — the most detailed, the darkest, the one with text in it. If that survives, the rest will.

Keep the originals

This is the part people regret skipping. A batch operation applies the same decision to dozens of files at once, so a bad decision is dozens of mistakes.

Snug writes results as new files in Pictures/Snug and never touches the originals, which means a batch you are unhappy with costs you nothing but the time to run it again.

What the numbers usually look like

A typical holiday album — a dozen photos at 3–5 MB each — comes to around 48 MB. Resized for sharing, the same twelve land near 9 or 10 MB in total, an 80% saving, and on a phone screen you would be hard pressed to tell which is which.

That is the difference between an email that sends and one that bounces.

The short version

Decide the destination, test on your hardest photo, then run the batch. Originals stay untouched, so a bad call costs only time.
