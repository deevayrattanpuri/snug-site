Why online forms reject your photo (and how to fix each reason)

"Invalid image" is the least helpful error message in existence. These are the eight things it usually means.

Sizing 6 October 2026 ·7 min read

Upload forms are built by people who had a very specific file in mind, and they rarely explain what it was. You get Invalid image or Upload failed, with no indication which rule you broke. Here are the usual suspects, roughly in the order they catch people out.

1. The file is too big

The most common one, and the easiest to fix. The limit is almost always stated in kilobytes somewhere near the upload button. Resize the dimensions down first, then compress — in that order — and you will usually clear the limit without the photo looking mangled.

2. The file is too small

Less common but genuinely confusing when it happens. Some portals set a minimum size, because a 12 KB image is probably too low-resolution to verify anything. If you have over-compressed, go back to the original and try again with a higher ceiling.

3. The dimensions are wrong

A form asking for 600 × 600 means exactly that, not "about square". If your photo is 4032 × 3024, no amount of compression will satisfy it. Crop to the right shape first, then set the exact pixel dimensions.

4. The aspect ratio is wrong

Subtly different from the above. Some forms accept a range of sizes but insist on a ratio — 35:45 for many passport photos, 1:1 for profile pictures. Resizing a 4:3 photo into a square without cropping will stretch faces, and some systems detect it.

5. The format is not supported

This is the HEIC problem. Modern iPhones and many Android cameras save HEIC by default, and a large number of upload forms have never heard of it. The file looks like a photo on your phone and arrives as an unreadable blob. Convert to JPG and it works.

WebP causes the same trouble on older systems, and PNG is occasionally rejected by forms that specifically want a photograph.

6. The orientation is wrong

A maddening one. Photos store a rotation flag in their metadata, and your gallery honours it, so the picture looks upright. A form that strips metadata reads the raw pixels and gets a sideways image. Rotating and re-saving bakes the correct orientation into the pixels themselves.

7. Metadata is confusing the validator

Some strict validators reject files with unexpected colour profiles or embedded thumbnails. Stripping metadata produces a plain, boring file that almost nothing objects to — and has the side benefit of removing the GPS coordinates.

8. The content does not meet the rules

The one no resizer can solve. Document photos are checked for background colour, head position, shadows, expression and whether you are wearing glasses. If the file is mechanically perfect and still rejected, re-read the content rules rather than the technical ones.

A sensible order to work through

  1. Read the stated requirements again, properly, and write down every number.
  2. Convert to JPG unless told otherwise.
  3. Crop to the required ratio.
  4. Set the exact pixel dimensions.
  5. Bring the file under the size ceiling.
  6. Strip the metadata.

Six steps, and the first one solves more cases than the other five combined.

The short version

Convert, crop, resize, compress, strip — in that order. And read the requirements twice before touching the photo.
