Why resizing on your phone beats uploading to a website

Free online resizers are convenient and they require you to hand a stranger your photograph first.

Privacy 2 June 2026 ·5 min read

Search for a photo resizer and you get a page of websites offering to do it free. Drag the file in, wait, download the result. It works. The question worth asking is what happened in between.

What an upload actually involves

To resize your photo, a website must receive your photo. Your file leaves the device, travels across the network, lands on a server owned by someone you have never met, is processed there, and is stored for some period you are not told about.

For a landscape photo that is unremarkable. For a passport photo, a signature, a bank statement, a medical form or a photograph of your child, it is a meaningful decision — and it is being made casually, because the interface makes it feel like nothing happened.

The questions you cannot answer

  • How long is the file kept after processing?
  • Is it deleted, or just unlinked from a URL that still works?
  • Who has access on their side?
  • Is it backed up, and for how long?
  • What happens to it if the company is sold or shut down?
  • What was in the privacy policy you did not read?

Reputable services have reasonable answers. The point is that you cannot verify any of them, and you are usually uploading before you have even looked.

The documents are the risky ones

It is worth being specific, because the risk is not evenly spread. The photos people most often need to resize urgently are exactly the sensitive ones: identity documents for an application, a signature for a form, proof of address, medical paperwork, a child's photo for a school portal.

These are the files you would never email to a stranger, and uploading them to an unknown server is closer to that than it feels.

On-device processing removes the question

A phone has had more than enough computing power to resize an image for over a decade. There is no technical reason the work has to happen elsewhere.

When it happens on the device, the whole category of questions disappears. There is no retention policy to evaluate because nothing was transmitted. Snug has no server at all — there is nothing to upload a photo to, which is also why it works with the network switched off entirely.

A simple test

Put the phone in airplane mode and try to do the job. If the tool still works, the photo never left. If it does not, you now know exactly where your file was going.

Convenience is not the trade-off it looks like

The usual assumption is that on-device means more effort. In practice it is faster: no upload wait, no download, no connection required, and it works on a train with one bar of signal. The convenience argument for uploading disappeared once phones got fast.

The short version

Airplane mode is the test. If it still works, your photo never left the device.
